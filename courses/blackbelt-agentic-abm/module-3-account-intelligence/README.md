# Account Intelligence

*Module 03 · Account Intelligence*

How to research a target account and its stakeholders properly: four levels of intelligence, where each level of information actually lives, and how to capture all of it in one working document — the account plan — before you ever try to personalize an offer.

## Why research runs top-down: the cascade

The class opens with a single idea that everything else in the module hangs off: personalized communication only works if you understand a chain of cause and effect, not just a single fact about one person.

- **Trends in an industry** cause **initiatives inside a company**.
- **Initiatives inside a company** cause **projects inside a department** — this is where a generic company initiative turns into one persona's actual job.
- **A project inside a department** becomes **one named person's task** — the thing a decision-maker is actually judged on this quarter.

Skip a link in that chain and personalization turns into guesswork: you can flatter an individual's ego without ever connecting to what their company or department actually needs from them, and it reads as generic no matter how much you know about their hobbies.

## The four levels of account intelligence

The module organizes every research task into four levels, from broadest to most personal. Each level answers a different question, and — this matters when you're deciding how much research budget an account deserves — each level takes progressively more effort per unit of insight.

| Level | What it answers | Typical inputs |
|---|---|---|
| Industry | What's reshaping the whole market this account competes in? | PEST(EL) analysis, Porter's Five Forces, trade press, analyst reports, regulatory filings |
| Company | What is this specific company doing about it? | Demography (revenue, headcount, locations), ownership structure, key initiatives, recent events, engagement signals already in your CRM |
| Department / persona | What does that mean for one team inside the company? | That department's goals and KPIs, its budget politics, who actually holds power in its buying decisions |
| Individual | What does that mean for one named person? | Role, tenure, career goals, fears, communication style, contact details |

Most sales failures, per the instructors, trace back to skipping straight to the bottom: pitching an individual without ever connecting the pitch to their company's actual priorities.

## Where to find it: four source categories

The module groups every research source into four categories, roughly in order of how much friction each one takes:

#### 1. Open sources

Internal (your own CRM/ERP history with the account), public (company website, press mentions, presentations, articles), and paid (industry analyst research, specialist B2B data providers). This is the default starting point for every account, regardless of tier.

#### 2. Your own sales team

A structured debrief with whoever on your side has already touched this account — an AE, a CSM, a BDR. Useful questions: What are the client's current problems and priorities, as they've stated them? What's their internal culture like? What's the status of any projects already running with this client? Which of our strengths does the client actually notice? Which competitors are active here, and why did they win or lose the last deal? Is there visibility into budget for similar initiatives? Where have we been most successful with this client, and why?

#### 3. The discovery call

The client, directly. Don't start at the top of the org chart — a straight answer is usually easier to get lower down. Everything from the sales-team debrief, plus: can you confirm these are your business goals, as far as I understand them? Our information suggests your main problems are X — which of those is most critical right now? What results do you expect from a solution like ours? What are your actual selection criteria for a vendor, and why those?

#### 4. Non-traditional sources — and where the line sits

The original training also covered paid qualitative research, mystery-shopping your own competitors, and interviews with current or former employees — all legitimate market-research techniques used carefully. It went further into impersonation-based tactics too: fake tenders to extract competitor pricing, pretexting phone calls to identify a decision-maker, and scraping personal phone numbers off social platforms. We're not reproducing the how-to for any of that here. In the US, UK and EU those tactics risk real legal exposure under GDPR, UK GDPR, CCPA and computer-fraud statutes, not just an ethics question — and the simplest working rule is: if a tactic depends on the target not knowing who you are or what you're collecting, don't run it.

## Company-level research: key initiatives are the hook

A **key initiative** is a project outside day-to-day operations meant to change the company or its results — usually running 3-5 years, with a name and a measurable "from A to B" target. Common shapes: grow revenue and profitability, improve product quality, diversify the product line, increase customer lifetime value, cut costs to protect margin, move from a capex-heavy to an opex-heavy business model, scale into new regions. The instructors are blunt about the implication: your offer needs to visibly move the needle on one of an account's top three to five key initiatives — being generically relevant to the company isn't enough to earn budget.

The module's worked template for turning a company initiative into a pitch runs through six fields:

| Field | Worked example |
|---|---|
| Business imperative | Employees expect secure access to company systems from any device, anywhere, anytime. |
| Client initiative | Project "Anywhere Workforce" — give every consultant remote-office-grade access to core systems from any location. |
| Target audience | HR, IT and Finance leaders who know flexibility now equals productivity — but whose infrastructure wasn't built for it. |
| Our solution | A fully managed IT service supporting remote work on any device, billed on usage, with real-time reporting. |
| Benefits if they choose us | Better cash flow (opex not capex), IT freed from break-fix work, instant scaling with headcount changes. |
| Why us, not a competitor | Already running for 75 companies and 150,000 employees globally, on security-first infrastructure. |

Once you've listed 3-7 key initiatives for an account, the module has you build an **opportunity matrix**: every product or service line you sell, mapped against every initiative, marking which combinations have a real story. Empty rows are accounts you can't yet justify chasing at this tier; empty columns are offers with no current initiative to attach to.

Before pitching any initiative, the module's self-check runs: What are the account's 3-7 key initiatives, including ones that don't touch you directly? Which can your offer realistically attach to? Who owns each initiative internally (often visible in annual reports, or from a conversation with a junior contact)? How is success on it measured and rewarded? Which parts of the organization does it actually touch — rarely just one department? And can you prove, not just assert, why your offer is necessary to its success?

## Persona and individual-level research

Once you're past the company level, the module has you build a profile of each stakeholder in two parts. **Profile** — the "who": photo (so you recognize them in person), job title and tenure, previous roles, education, professional associations, talks and published articles, and contact information. **Psychographics** — the "what do they want": personal needs, career goals and what they want recognized, fears and perceived risks, and how they'll be personally judged on this decision.

The five most effective ways the module lists for opening a conversation with a decision-maker, each illustrated with a fictional example in the style the original training used:

- **Content they authored** — "Priya, I read your piece on vendor consolidation, and the point about hidden integration cost really landed…"
- **Content they engaged with** — "Marcus, I saw your comment on David Reyes' post about renewal pricing — sounds like that's a live issue for you too…"
- **How they describe themselves** — "Sarah, your profile says developing people is what you care about most — what's the biggest blocker to that this year?"
- **Personal interests, hobbies, alma mater** — a genuine, specific reference beats a generic compliment every time.
- **Their company's own news** — "Congratulations on the 40% growth last quarter — I imagine that's put real strain on onboarding?"

### The Western contact-data stack, July 2026

The original training's list of contact-finding services was almost entirely Russian-market tools; the table below is the Western-market equivalent, grouped the same way, verified current as of this course revision:

| Category | Tools |
|---|---|
| Company & contact data | ZoomInfo, Apollo.io, Crunchbase |
| Email finding & verification | Hunter.io, Snov.io, Lusha |
| People search & waterfall enrichment | Clay, RocketReach, SignalHire |
| Social & sales intelligence | LinkedIn Sales Navigator, Breeze Intelligence (formerly Clearbit, now part of HubSpot), BuiltWith |

No single tool wins every category, which is why most teams chain two or three into a "waterfall" — a miss on one provider gets picked up by the next. The module also covers boolean / X-ray search (combining `site:`, `company:` and job-title terms to search a specific platform, e.g. `site:linkedin.com/in "VP Marketing" "Acme Corp"`) — standard public-web search craft, not an exotic technique.

## Evaluating stakeholders and building the account plan

Once you have a list of stakeholders, the module has you score each one on three axes:

- **Role in the decision** — *economic buyer* (signs off on spend; there's exactly one per deal), *user* (lives with the result day to day; usually several, but at least one must be engaged), *technical buyer / gatekeeper* (owns spec fit; a professional "no" — they can kill a deal but rarely control budget), and *coach* (guides you through the account and shares information — they don't walk in the door on their own; you have to find and develop one).
- **Attitude toward you** — an eleven-point scale from -5 (Antagonistic Antisponsor) through 0 to +5 (Enthusiastic Advocate), borrowed directly from classic strategic-selling scorecards.
- **Degree of contact** — none, brief, several touches, or constantly in touch.

Laying every scored stakeholder out visually — reporting lines, who influences whom, where the coverage gaps are — produces a **relationship map**. Any simple diagramming tool works; the value is in forcing the team to say out loud who on the buying committee nobody has actually spoken to yet.

Everything above finally lands in one working document, the **account plan**. The module's own template (translated and downloadable above) runs through: account overview and relationship history; market trends, risks and regulatory change; product/service portfolio versus named competitors; goals and key initiatives with an account-specific Power SWOT; the buying committee as an org structure (roles, not just names); stakeholder scorecards per the axes above; decision criteria versus competitors; the opportunity matrix from the company-level section; and a 6-month strategy, timeline, content plan and partner list.

One planning number worth budgeting around: the module puts strategic (1:1) account research at roughly 10-12 hours per account, versus about 3 hours for a cluster (1:few) account — and separately notes that outbound prospecting typically takes about 12 calls to reach someone, 6 emails to get a reply, and 3 iterations to book a meeting, for a rough average of 75 calls or 40 emails per booked meeting. Set BDR capacity expectations against that math, not against how many accounts you'd like covered.

## 3.6 Agentic ABM in practice

Two patterns from the class, adapted to how account research actually gets done with AI help in 2026:

- **MegaBrain as the research agent** — a single prompt ("build a stakeholder map and recent-news brief for this target account"), routed through MegaBrain's model-agnostic Gateway, returns a structured first-draft account plan: industry trends, company initiatives, and a starting stakeholder list, with sources cited so a human can verify before anything goes near a prospect.
- **Other agent tools mapped to account-research tasks** — Clay to chain multiple contact-data providers into one enrichment waterfall for a stakeholder list; LinkedIn Sales Navigator paired with an AI copilot to turn saved searches and alerts into a drafted account brief; a deep-research web agent (Perplexity- or ChatGPT-style deep-research mode) to summarize a target industry's 2026 trends with citations in minutes; and Fireflies.ai or Otter.ai to auto-summarize a discovery call straight into the same fields the account plan needs, without manual note-taking.

## Exercises

#### Homework 1 — build one full account plan

Take the #1-ranked account from your Module 2 shortlist. Fill in the account overview and relationship-history sections using open sources — company site, news coverage, any public filings. Identify 3-5 key initiatives for that account and write one line on how your offer could attach to each. Map at least 5 real stakeholders with role, an estimated attitude score, and degree of contact. Run the opportunity matrix from the company-level section against your own product or service lines. Have a colleague or manager sanity-check the plan for gaps within a week.

#### Homework 2 — run one real discovery call or sales debrief

Book 30 minutes with your own AE, CSM or BDR who has touched this account — or with the client directly if you already have a live relationship. Bring the question sets from the "where to find it" section: the sales-team version if it's internal, the discovery-call version if it's the client. Write the answers straight into your account plan's "situation" and "key initiatives" fields, not into a separate notes document. Flag anything that contradicts what you assumed from open sources — that gap is usually the most useful single finding of the exercise.

#### Homework 3 — build your contact-data stack

Pick two tools from the July 2026 landscape table above that cover different categories — for example, LinkedIn Sales Navigator (from Module 1's Homework 3) plus Hunter.io or Apollo.io. Sign up for the free tier or trial of each. Enrich the stakeholder list from Homework 1: find a verified work email and LinkedIn URL for at least 5 people. Run at least 5 of the emails you find through a verification tool before you consider them usable.

#### Homework 4 — run an agent-built account brief through MegaBrain

Using the MegaBrain account you set up in Module 1's Homework 4, write a research prompt for your Homework-1 account's industry: ask for recent trends, regulatory changes, and 5 named industry voices with sources. Run a second prompt asking specifically for that account's public key initiatives and who owns each one. Paste both outputs into the relevant sections of your account plan, and verify every factual claim against its cited source before you trust it. Note where the agent's draft was genuinely useful versus where it guessed — bring both examples to the Module 4 session.
