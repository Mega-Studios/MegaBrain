# Fundamentals of ABM

*Module 01 · Fundamentals*

Where Account-Based Marketing came from, why "more leads" stopped being the answer for enterprise B2B, and what actually changes when marketing and sales run a program against a named list of accounts instead of a funnel of anonymous leads.

> This page replaces the recorded session for Module 1. There's no transcript — the original video wasn't processed — so treat this as written module notes covering the same ground the class does, not a word-for-word record of what was said live.

## A short history of B2B sales and marketing

The class opens with a timeline, not a definition, because ABM is easier to place once you've seen what it replaced. The rough arc:

- **1860s–1920s** — the earliest documented "sales" playbooks: snake-oil selling, N.C.R.'s pyramid selling, the first mail-order catalog, trust selling, brand-based selling.
- **1930s** — barrier selling and mood selling, still fundamentally: convince the person in front of you.
- **1950s–1970s** — need-satisfaction selling, then relationship selling: the idea that a sale follows from understanding a buyer's actual problem, not from technique.
- **1978** — Miller-Heiman's Strategic Selling, one of the first systematic approaches to multi-stakeholder enterprise deals.
- **1986–1988** — ACT!, the first mainstream CRM, and SPIN Selling, a consultative, question-led method.
- **1991** — value-added selling: "cradle to grave," stay with the customer past the sale.
- **1993** — Amazon starts selling online; the buyer starts doing their own research.
- **1995** — MEDDIC (Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, Champion) — a qualification checklist still in wide use.
- **2000** — Solution Selling: build a shared view of the best solution with the buyer, rather than pitching a product at them.
- **2010s** — the "new school" of B2B marketing arrives: SEO, content marketing, email nurture, paid social, marketing automation, and sales funnels tuned around inbound lead volume.
- **2011** — two ideas land in the same year that matter a lot for this course: the Challenger Sale (control the conversation, teach the customer something new) and Aaron Ross's *Predictable Revenue*, which splits the funnel into Marketing → Business Development Rep → Account Executive — the ancestor of the modern SDR/BDR function.

### Go-to-market strategies, side by side

By contract size, the deck lines up five go-to-market motions from cheapest-and-widest to most-expensive-and-narrowest:

| Motion | Typical annual contract value | Deal volume |
|---|---|---|
| Product-Led Growth | ~$100 | Thousands |
| Inbound lead generation | ~$2,000 | Hundreds |
| ABM 1:many | ~$5,000 | Tens |
| ABM 1:few (cluster) | ~$25,000–$250,000 | Handful |
| ABM 1:1 (strategic) | ~$500,000+ | Single digits |

The point isn't that one motion is "better" — it's that contract size and account count should tell you which motion you're actually running. Most of the failure mode this module describes is applying an inbound, high-volume motion to accounts that only make sense as 1:few or 1:1.

## Why inbound alone breaks down for enterprise accounts

Inbound's "new school" playbook — content, SEM/SEO, social, webinars, retargeting, email nurture — is built around funneling an anonymous individual through MQL → SAL → SQL → CRM opportunity. It works well when the buying unit is one person with a credit card. It works much worse when:

- The real buying group is 5–15 people across several departments, and a lead-scored individual is a poor proxy for "the account is ready."
- The number of accounts that could plausibly buy is small (dozens, not thousands) — "spray and pray" lead generation is a luxury you don't have when the addressable list is short.
- Marketing optimizes for lead volume while sales needs qualified conversations inside a handful of named companies — the classic "throwing leads over the fence" complaint.

Two supporting ideas the module borrows from behavioral science and B2C/B2B contrast:

- **SCARF (David Rock, 2009)** — Status, Certainty, Autonomy, Relatedness, Fairness: five levers of social threat/reward that predict how a stakeholder reacts to a pitch. The practical note: don't design only for the rational, "prefrontal cortex" reading of a deal — the threat response (amygdala) is often what actually decides.
- **B2C buyers regret bad purchases; B2B buyers get blamed for them** — "nobody ever got fired for buying IBM." Risk aversion, not just ROI, drives enterprise buying committees.

None of this argues against brand marketing or inbound — the deck is explicit that **ABM doesn't replace brand marketing**. Thought leadership, executive relationship programs, community-building, referral/word-of-mouth programs, and analyst relations still matter; ABM is what you add on top when a short list of accounts is worth a dedicated, coordinated push.

## What Account-Based Marketing is

Account-Based Marketing is a customer-centric go-to-market model where marketing and sales work as one team against a defined account or micro-segment, rather than against a pool of leads. Two things follow from that definition:

- **Multi-threaded by design** — the program engages every relevant decision-maker inside the account at once, not one contact at a time.
- **Personalized by tier** — offer and content are tailored at the industry, company, and persona/department level (Module 5 goes deep on this).

A single account journey typically runs: **Targeted → Active (MQA) → Prioritized/Engaged → Qualified → Deal pipeline → Won client**, using a channel mix that's much broader than inbound's toolbox — social selling, direct mail, 1:1 events, C-level roundtables, business breakfasts, and video messages sit alongside the usual email and advertising. And unlike a lead funnel that ends at "closed won," the ABM account lifecycle continues into onboarding, renewal, upsell/cross-sell, and advocacy — the same coordinated, cross-functional approach that won the account is what expands it.

## Three tiers of ABM program

The module treats "ABM" as three genuinely different programs, sized to how many accounts you're running at once — not one methodology with a volume knob.

#### 1:1 — Strategic ABM (1–10 accounts)

Fully bespoke programs, each account run almost like its own micro-business unit — custom research, custom content, executive sponsorship, often a dedicated account team. This is the tier for the handful of accounts where the potential contract value justifies near-unlimited personalization.

#### ABM Lite — clustered (10–50 accounts)

Shared plays across a segment with light personalization at the account level. The module walks through four published examples at this tier:

- **OneLogin** — a textbook-clean pipeline: define ABM program goals (ARR, win rate, average deal size) → build an ICP → tier accounts by intent → build personas and insight decks → arm sales with content (articles, case studies, spec sheets, email templates, decks) → run and monitor weekly.
- **Schneider** — used a LinkedIn group and a co-branded market research report ("State of the mid-market supply chain") as the wedge, then moved warm respondents to 1:1 outreach.
- **Trelleborg** — built a content plan around the customer journey with subject-matter experts, then targeted and enriched a contact list against it. Reported results: 5,216 landing-page visits (half from target accounts), 17.3% multi-open email rate, $6M in marketing-sourced pipeline.
- **Boon Edam** — a short, scripted multi-touch sequence: Day 1 social touch → Day 3 3D direct mail piece → Day 5 call ("did you get it? here's a PDF and checklist") → Day 6 executive email inviting to breakfast → Day 15 breakfast event → Day 15 follow-up email → in-person meeting to scope an audit.

#### ABM Programmatic (50–500 accounts)

Intent data and account-based advertising replace hand-built sequences at this volume — the same channel list (social selling, direct mail, content hubs, matchmaking/intro services, chatbots, sales and marketing email, video email, offline events, webinars, content syndication) but triggered by scored intent signals rather than a human picking the next touch. Example: a real-estate marketing research program that moved 570 accounts through a landing page, a business breakfast (96 attendees), and two follow-up webinars (80 and 60 attendees).

> Class exercise from this section: is a business-loan sales campaign actually ABM, or just well-run outbound? The useful part isn't the verdict — it's the diagnostic questions: is there a named, finite account list; is the messaging personalized past "Hi {{first_name}}"; are marketing and sales actually coordinating on the same accounts. Run any campaign you're evaluating through the same three questions.

## Where ABM meets inbound

A hybrid pattern shows up repeatedly in modern stacks: run inbound as normal, but de-anonymize which *company* is visiting the site (not the individual) and route that signal into the ABM motion. Tools named in class for this: HubSpot, Factors.ai, Albacross, Dealfront, and Koala. This is also where the two funnels — inbound's MQL/SAL/SQL and ABM's Targeted/Active/Engaged — start to converge into one combined 2018-era "dual funnel."

## 2025 update: LLMs and AI agents in the ABM toolkit

This section was added to the training after ChatGPT's November 2022 launch and covers the practical end of generative AI for ABM work, not the theory:

- **LLMs** — large language models, neural networks trained on huge text corpora, capable of understanding and generating text across a wide range of tasks, from Q&A to code.
- **Agents** — a system with two parts: a "brain" (the LLM, doing the reasoning and planning) and a "body" (the tools and actions it's allowed to take). What an agent can actually do is bounded by the tools it's given, not by the model alone.
- **Applied tools surveyed in class**: Clay (data enrichment and outreach workflows), Relevance AI (agent building), Bardeen (browser automation), sandboxed/no-code agent builders like Make and n8n, and spreadsheet-native AI (Sheet Magic). The point of the survey isn't to endorse a specific vendor — the list turns over constantly — it's to establish the categories: enrichment, outreach automation, browser/workflow automation, and general-purpose agent building.

> A 2025 framing quoted in class: "we now know how to build real AI agents... in 2025 the first AI agents will work alongside people and meaningfully affect business... if you give people good tools, they can accomplish a lot more." Treat this as a snapshot of where the instructors' thinking was, not as a permanent claim about the state of AI.

## Worked example: scoring and shortlisting for a B2B shared-services provider

The in-class capstone example (anonymized here; the original used a real client's internal account list) walks a shared-services/BPO provider through the full loop in miniature:

- A scoring model plotted on a GE/McKinsey-style 2x2 — *account attractiveness* (growth, headcount, segment, region) against *our competitive position* — used to rank roughly 25 candidate accounts and cut the list to a short set of named targets.
- Five target personas defined for the shortlist: CFO/financial controller/chief accountant, HR director, managing director/transformation lead, procurement director, and shared-services-center director.
- A full persona brief for the CFO persona: their day-to-day pains (manual, slow month-end close; stuck doing routine work instead of strategy; high per-employee payroll cost), likely objections (loss of control, fear of errors, "our accounting is too specific for an outsourcer to learn," data handed to a third party), and a content/CTA ladder mapped to funnel stage — interest-stage articles and a content hub, consideration-stage webinars and a savings calculator, decision-stage case demos and audit conversations.
- A contact-list template for capturing target buying-committee members — *illustrative fields only*: first name, last name, company, job title, mobile, email, LinkedIn. The original training material populated this with real employees' names, personal emails, and phone numbers at named companies; that data isn't reproduced here for privacy reasons — use the field structure, not real people, when you build your own.
- A KPI plan for a 20-account strategic program tracking contacts-with-email-in-database, engaged contacts, meetings booked, director-level meetings, "tripwire" workshops/demos, opportunities and pipeline value in CRM, and signed contracts — each with a now / 1-month / 3-month / 6-month target.
- A campaign budget and RACI-style execution plan breaking a lead-magnet-plus-webinar campaign into ~228 hours of named tasks (social profile setup, article drafts, landing pages, webinar prep, assessment methodology, proposal template) against a calendar.

## Exercises

#### Homework 1 — best and worst customers

Pull the domain names of your existing (not target) customers straight from your CRM or billing system — not from memory. Rank them best-to-worst by feel, not a fixed formula: revenue, low support burden, how much you enjoy working with them. Write down the best 10–15, then keep ranking past them until quality clearly drops off, and mark that cutoff line. Repeat the same exercise for your worst 10–15 existing customers. Bring both lists to Module 2 — they're the seed data for the Ideal Customer Profile you'll build there.

#### Homework 2 — website visitor identification

Pick one company-level visitor-ID tool (HubSpot, Factors.ai, Albacross, Dealfront, or Koala — see the "where ABM meets inbound" section above). Install its tracking script on your site and let it run for at least a full week of real traffic. Calculate what share of total visitors it actually resolves to a named company, then look at the resolved companies and flag which ones look like attractive target accounts — and write down why.

#### Homework 3 — LinkedIn Sales Navigator

Start a free trial at linkedin.com/sales/onboarding. Build one saved account search and one saved lead search matching the customer profile from Homework 1 — you'll use this trial directly for account and stakeholder research in Module 3.

#### Homework 4 — set up MegaBrain

Create a free account at [getmegabrain.com](https://getmegabrain.com) — no credit card required, free models are available immediately. Generate an API key from the dashboard and send one test request through the Gateway. This one isn't optional: Module 5's content and personalization exercises are built around having a working MegaBrain account.
