# ABM Metrics

*Module 04 · ABM Metrics*

Three groups of metrics, the waterfall funnel by tier, and how to prove an ABM program is working months before any deal actually closes.

> This page replaces the recorded session for Module 4. There's no transcript — the original video wasn't processed — so treat this as written module notes covering the same ground as the class, not a word-for-word record of what was said live. A few source slides showed a real client's actual named target accounts and a real contact map with real individuals' names; those are described here in generic, anonymized form rather than reproduced, and dollar figures are presented as illustrative USD.

## Why "leads" is the wrong word

The session opens by taking a swing at the vocabulary most B2B marketing teams inherited from inbound: new leads, MQLs, click-through rate. The training calls these "vanity metrics" for one blunt reason — **you don't sign a contract with a lead, you sign it with a company.** A line quoted in class from David Ogilvy makes the same point from the other direction: don't count the people you reach — reach the people who count. The practical fix isn't to stop measuring; it's to stop measuring individual, anonymous activity and start measuring account-level progress across three groups of metrics the training calls the **3R framework**:

- **Revenue** — the funnel: conversions and conversion speed, quotation and opportunity value, ROI and lifetime value.
- **Relations** — coverage and engagement: are the right decision-makers in a target account actually paying attention, and how much.
- **Reputation** — trust: NPS, brand perception, and whether you're developing genuine champions inside the account.

Everything else in this module is one of those three buckets, plus how to prove progress before the Revenue numbers exist yet.

## Case study: JLL's pilot ABM program

JLL — a commercial real-estate and investment-management firm — ran a pilot ABM program against 13 target accounts: 10 new-logo targets and 3 existing clients up for expansion. The published results, cited in class as a worked example of setting and then beating a plan:

- **Step 1 — demand map:** the 13 accounts broke down into 49 distinct buying centers representing roughly **$21.2M** of addressable potential.
- **Step 2 — contact discovery:** account plans, sales notes, NPS surveys and CRM data combined to validate **144 contacts** across those buying centers, split between decision-makers and influencers. (The source deck included a real contact map with real individuals' names at a real account — not reproduced here; build your own using a generic contact-map template: name, role, department, relationship strength, and whether they're an advocate, neutral, or detractor.)
- **Step 3 — a tailored program** per client and buying center: customized rep-written introduction emails and personalized landing pages, 3D direct mail to key target directors with follow-up calls, drip email nurture sequences, IP-targeted banner advertising, and topical webinars.
- **Step 5 — measure and optimize:** six months in, actuals beat plan on every tracked metric — new buying centers engaged per account, share of decision-makers engaged, share of buying centers with at least one engaged decision-maker, qualified deals and their value in the pipeline, and signed contract value.
- **Scaling the next year:** the program expanded to 62 target clients, 582 contacts, 129 new opportunities identified, and roughly $53.6M in identified pipeline.

The shape of this case — map demand, validate contacts, run a tailored program, measure against a plan, then scale — is the template the rest of the module unpacks metric group by metric group.

## Reputation — the trust metrics

Reputation metrics track whether a target account is starting to see you as a partner rather than one vendor among many. The training frames this as a loyalty ladder, using a share-of-wallet (SoW) lens:

| Stage | Share of wallet | Purchase pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Prospect | 0% | 0 purchases in the trailing 36 months |
| Buying | <10% | 1 purchase in the trailing 36 months |
| Active | ≥20% | 2 purchases in the trailing 12 months |
| Frequent | ≥30% | 4 purchases in the trailing 12 months |
| Engaged | >50% | 12 purchases in the trailing 12 months; joint innovation/market planning |

### Champion, Influencer, or Coach?

A recurring qualification exercise (referencing *The Challenger Sale*'s vocabulary) asks whether a contact who seems friendly is actually useful. A genuine **champion** has all three of:

- **Incentive** — a personal stake or reward tied to your deal succeeding.
- **Influence** — real social capital with other stakeholders, and the willingness to spend it advancing your case.
- **Information** — they help you navigate the account and avoid landmines.

Influence + information without incentive is an **Influencer** — helpful, but not personally invested. Incentive + information without influence is a **Coach** — invested and informative, but can't move the room. Four field tests to tell them apart: ask to switch to a more personal channel (WhatsApp, text) and see if they hesitate; ask them to describe their own internal buying process and see how freely they map it; make a small ask (an intro, feedback on a deck) and see if they follow through; and ask them to explain your pitch back to you as if presenting it internally, then offer to role-play the objections they'd face.

> **Exercise from class (anonymized):** calculate an NPS score from a table of respondents and their 0-10 scores. NPS = % Promoters (9-10) − % Detractors (0-6). Try it yourself with any 10-15 real scores you have on hand — the original exercise used real individuals' names, replaced here with "Respondent A, B, C…" since the scores themselves aren't personal data but the names are.

**Reputation — possible metrics:** number of champions/coaches developed per account; awareness of the account's internal strategic initiatives; brand-perception trend and target-account website traffic; thought-leadership content downloads (opt-ins); campaign response rate; satisfaction and NPS; loyalty-ladder stage over time; references given and case-study consent.

## Relations — coverage and engagement

Relations metrics answer a narrower question than Reputation: not "do they like us" but "are the right people actually paying attention, and how much." Two building blocks the training uses:

### Coverage

Map every known contact in a target account against hierarchy level (staff → manager → head → director → VP/C-suite) and department. The gap in that grid — the departments and levels you have zero contacts in — is your outreach to-do list, not a metric to report as-is.

### Engagement — attribution in minutes

To turn "some contacts are more engaged than others" into a number, assign every touch type a weight (illustrative, adapt to your own funnel): a demo is worth far more attention than an email open. Two published real-world versions of this idea, cited in class: **Grant Thornton's** points-based marketing-engagement score (web visits, email opens/clicks, event attendance, NPS-linked bonuses and penalties), and **Localytics'** engagement-minutes model, which multiplies raw minutes by a role multiplier — a CXO's minute is worth 5x an individual contributor's. Sum the weighted minutes per contact and per account, and you get an **MQA** (Marketing Qualified Account): the account has earned enough engagement to justify handing it to your most experienced closers instead of a generic sequence.

A Customer Data Platform (CDP) is the usual place to operationalize this at scale — it unifies contact and company data from CRM, website, and offline sources into one profile, then feeds segmentation, intent scoring, and trigger-based personalization back out. You don't need one to start: a spreadsheet and the weighting table above will get you through your first 20-50 target accounts.

### The dark funnel

A caution the training borrows from marketing analyst Chris Walker: attribution models built only on trackable digital touches keep losing accuracy, because more of the real buying journey happens where you can't see it — competitor research, an anonymous site visit, word-of-mouth, an internal Slack thread, a colleague's recommendation, a community discussion. Don't over-trust last-touch or first-touch attribution alone; triangulate with self-reported "how did you hear about us" answers and directional signals like branded search volume alongside whatever you can track directly.

**Relations — possible metrics:** new target-account contacts added to the database (especially at director level and above); engaged contacts/directors; meetings, demos, audits, and feasibility studies held; directors participating in advisory councils; subjective engagement-strength score per contact; attribution-weighted minutes and MQA status per account.

## Revenue — the funnel

The funnel itself has evolved twice in recent memory — SiriusDecisions' 2017 model and Forrester's 2021 model both tried to reconcile a demand-generation funnel with an account-based one — but the practical point for this module is simpler: **your target accounts should out-perform your non-target accounts on the same funnel**, or the program isn't earning its keep. An illustrative comparison in the style used in class:

| Cohort | Avg. deal size | Win rate | Deal length | Retention | NPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| New target accounts | $35,500 | 35% | 187 days | 79% | 32 |
| Current target accounts | $42,500 | 42% | 95 days | 94% | 52 |
| Non-target accounts | $27,250 | 21% | 213 days | 74% | 11 |

If your target accounts don't clearly beat that control group, either the account list is wrong (Module 2) or the program isn't actually differentiated enough yet (Module 5 and 6).

### The waterfall funnel by tier

The most useful chart in this section is also the simplest: a stacked cohort count, tracking how many target accounts sit in each pipeline stage today, and where you expect that stack to shift over the next 1-3 months. An illustrative version, adapted from the source material's own goal-setting template:

| Stage | Now | +1 month | +2 months | +3 months |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold accounts | 112 | 80 | 60 | 50 |
| Established contact | 41 | 48 | 43 | 28 |
| Substantive meetings | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 |
| In negotiation | 28 | 38 | 48 | 58 |
| New contracts | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 |

This is exactly the shape the "Waterfall" tab of this module's KPI workbook is built around — fill in your own cohort counts and let the sheet total them.

**Revenue — possible metrics:** RFP/tender invitations from target accounts; number, growth rate and velocity of pipeline opportunities; new opportunities logged this period; portfolio penetration (products sold per account); stage-to-stage conversion and average cycle length; win rate and average contract value; target-account performance vs. a non-target control group.

## Proving progress before deals close

A signed contract can be 6-12+ months away, especially in the 1:1 strategic tier — and both program sponsors and executives tend to lose patience well before then. The training's answer is to define, per ABM tier, an earlier and honest proxy for "this is working":

#### Strategic 1:1 (1-10 accounts)

**Months 1-3:** charter signed, account research complete. **Months 4-6:** an internal workshop run, thought-leadership content built and stakeholders profiled. **Month 6+ success metric:** strategic conversations and meetings actually happening with key decision-makers — not revenue yet, but proof the relationship is real.

#### ABM Lite / cluster (10-50 accounts)

**Months 1-3:** the ABM plan is approved and "sold" internally, with dynamic landing pages ready for personalized content. **Months 4-6:** thought-leadership content is actively distributed through creative touchpoints and management-level relationships are forming. **Month 6+ success metric:** deals logged as opportunities in CRM.

#### Programmatic (50-500 accounts)

**Months 1-3:** the campaign and communication materials are built. **Months 4-6:** the campaign is live, nurturing and engaging accounts at scale. **Month 6+ success metric:** revenue — closed/won deals and contract value.

Whichever tier you're running, agree the tier-appropriate proxy metric with your sponsor *before* the program starts. It's much easier to get buy-in on what counts as progress before the numbers exist than to retrofit a definition of success after month three looks disappointing.

## Keep it simple

The session closes with a deliberately modest worked example — a small strategic program (a handful of target accounts, 1-2 people on it) tracked with a single table: contacts in the database, engaged contacts, meetings, "tripwire" workshops or demos, proof-of-concept count, deals and their value in CRM, and signed contracts — each with a now / +3-month / +6-month / +12-month target. That table is exactly what this module's KPI workbook reproduces, translated and rebuilt in USD. You don't need a dozen KPIs to run a defensible program; you need a handful, tracked consistently, shared with sales.

## Agentic ABM in practice

Two places an agent, not a person, can own the reporting grind:

- **The weekly pipeline-health brief.** Feed a CRM/engagement export to an agent through MegaBrain's gateway with a prompt like "summarize this week's ABM funnel movement and flag stalled accounts," and get back a written brief with next actions — the same synthesis a BI analyst or RevOps hire would otherwise produce by hand every Monday.
- **Other tools worth knowing for this specific job:** **Dreamdata** — a B2B revenue-attribution platform that has moved from pure multi-touch attribution into AI agents that turn that data into plain-language pipeline narratives; **Databox** — connects 130+ data sources into real-time KPI dashboards with an AI layer that surfaces summaries and forecasts; **HubSpot's Breeze Assistant** — CRM-native AI that drafts quick-look reports directly from data already sitting in HubSpot; and **Make or n8n** — a scheduled workflow that pulls the week's funnel movement and posts a digest to Slack every Monday morning, no BI tool required.

## Exercises

#### Homework 1 — build your own 3R metrics plan

Pick the ABM tier you're actually running (Strategic 1:1, ABM Lite/cluster, or Programmatic) and write down your target-account count for this program. For each of the three groups — Revenue, Relations, Reputation — choose 2-3 metrics from this module's "possible metrics" lists that you can actually measure with tools you have today. Set a target for each at four horizons: now, +3 months, +6 months, +12 months — round numbers are fine, precision isn't the point yet. Fill in the KPI workbook that ships with this module using your own numbers, not the illustrative example, and share the completed sheet with whoever owns sales for these accounts before your next planning session — a KPI plan nobody on the sales side has seen isn't a shared plan.

#### Homework 2 — build an attribution-in-minutes model

List every touch type your team actually uses today — calls, demos, webinars, direct mail, gated content downloads, email opens, meetings. Assign each one a weight (minutes or points; the unit doesn't matter, the relative ranking does), using this module's example as a starting point. Add a role multiplier so a CXO's engagement counts for more than an individual contributor's, the way Localytics' model does. Run the model by hand against 3-5 real target accounts using whatever CRM/analytics data you can pull this week, and flag which of them already clear your own MQA threshold — then send that shortlist to sales.

#### Homework 3 — map your own "proof before close" timeline

Using the tier-by-tier grid from the "proving progress" section as a template, write down what progress will concretely look like for your program at months 1-3, 4-6, and 6+. Be specific: not "more engagement" but "3 workshop-style calls booked with VP-or-above contacts at 5 of our 8 target accounts." Share this timeline with your manager or executive sponsor now, before the program starts. Put a check-in on the calendar at each milestone date to compare actuals against this plan — the same discipline the JLL case study used to demonstrate its results.

#### Homework 4 — run your first MegaBrain reporting brief

Export this week's account and opportunity data from your CRM (or reuse last homework's manual pull if you don't have full CRM access yet). Using the MegaBrain account you set up in Module 1, send a prompt asking the model to summarize funnel movement, flag stalled accounts, and suggest next actions. Compare the agent's brief against your own read of the same data — what did it get right, what did it miss or get wrong — and keep a running note of which prompts produce a brief you'd actually forward to your manager unedited.
