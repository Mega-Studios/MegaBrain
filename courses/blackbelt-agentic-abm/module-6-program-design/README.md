# Designing and Running the ABM Program

*Module 06 · Program design & activation*

Everything from Modules 2-5 — the account list, the account intelligence, the goals, the personalized offer — becomes real the moment you turn it into a scheduled sequence of touchpoints with a budget and a named owner per task. This module covers both halves of that: designing the program (warm-up, engagement, nurture, acceleration) and running it day to day (team roles, meeting cadence, keeping sales and marketing aligned).

## Nine ABM plays, one timed sequence

The module treats "program design" as assembling touchpoints into four timed phases that run for every account, then repeat after the first sale closes:

| Phase | Typical duration | Account-status shift |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | ~2 weeks | Target → Active |
| Engagement | ~2 weeks | Active → Engaged |
| Nurture | ~10-30 weeks | Engaged → MQA → SQA |
| Acceleration | ~4-8 weeks | SQA → Deal pipeline → Client |

Underneath the funnel status sits a second, more human ladder the class uses to describe the same progression: **Prospect → Buddy → Companion → Friend → Fan**. And once an account is won, the same four-phase machine runs again for **advocacy, up-sell/cross-sell, expansion, and renewal** — winning an account isn't the end of the program, it's a restart with a warmer list.

## Warm-up & prospecting

Before the first direct message, the goal is simply to be recognizable when it lands. The class groups warm-up tactics into six levers — pick two or three per account tier, not all six at once:

- **PR — speak and appear**: conference panels and speaking slots, but only at events where at least 30% of attendees are realistically target accounts.
- **PR — thought leadership**: bylined articles built around the target accounts' actual pains, naming and crediting those accounts' own expertise inside the piece — not just publishing about yourself.
- **PR — rankings and contests**: industry-analyst rankings for credibility, and contests aimed at target-account staff (not prospects in general) so the account's own people carry the win back into their organization.
- **Targeted advertising**: LinkedIn Campaign Manager and programmatic account-based advertising (ABA) run against an uploaded account match-list.
- **Social selling**: a personal brand and content plan built around four blocks — positioning, a content plan, growing the right network, and engaging before pitching.
- **Offline alternatives**: local or trade press, and sponsorships placed near the target account's own industry events, when a paid digital channel isn't available or isn't trusted locally.

A simple content-mix rule the class uses for a personal LinkedIn profile: roughly 50% expert/educational posts, 20% company or business news, and the rest split across personal posts, humor, and the occasional promotional post — spread across a 4-week calendar rather than clustered.

## Engagement — the first conversation

The goal of the first real touch is a reply, not a close. Ranked roughly by how well they convert to a first meeting, from best to worst: a warm referral, networking at an event or niche association, direct mail, social selling, email (a short video usually beats plain text), a cold call, and a paid ad into an opt-in landing page. Ask for the referral before you reach for the cold channel underneath it.

Whatever the channel, open with something real, not a template:

- Something they wrote, said, or presented — reference the actual idea, not just that you "saw it."
- Something they engaged with — a comment, a like, a repost — shows you're watching the right feed.
- What they say about themselves in their own profile or bio.
- Something about their company — a new hire, an expansion, a product launch — not just about them.
- A shared or niche interest works, but keep it close to their world of work, not yours.

Once the first message lands, a five-step follow-up cascade keeps the door open without nagging: an email on **day 1**, then follow-ups on **days 3, 5, 7, 12, and 21** — each one adding something new (a fresh insight, a relevant case, industry news), never just "following up on my last email."

## Nurture — keeping the account warm

This is the longest phase by far (10-30 weeks), and the job changes: stop trying to force a meeting and instead stay useful and visible until an intent signal says the account is ready. The class's nurture toolkit: email sequences and website chatbots, personalized content hubs, always-on retargeting, private online communities, on/offline events (webinars, meetups, roadshows), and meeting-scheduling tools that remove friction the moment someone is ready to talk. Run several in parallel and let signals — a content-hub revisit, a retargeting click, a community reply — tell you when to move an account into acceleration.

## Acceleration — winning the executive floor

The last mile needs a different playbook aimed specifically at the people who sign, ranked roughly by effectiveness: executive briefing centres, industry-association access, executive events and clubs, thought-leadership promotion, executive sponsorship programmes, business lunches and dinners, executive advisory boards, and reference/advocacy programmes. The pattern across all of them: 1:1 relationship building outperforms 1:many content at this stage. A useful format the class calls out specifically: collective-learning sessions or "innovation days" that open with the client's business problems, not your product — share best practices, sketch solution options together, and end with a loosely agreed roadmap and next step.

## Assembling the program: budget, timeline, RACI

Once the tactics are chosen, they still need to become a plan someone can actually run. Three components, in order:

#### RACI, agreed before week one

**R**esponsible (does the work), **A**ccountable (owns the outcome and signs off — exactly one person per task), **C**onsulted (weighs in before the decision), and **I**nformed (told after). Assign this up front — the point of RACI is to prevent the "I thought you had it" conversation after something slips, not to have it.

#### A calendar with hours per task

Break every tactic into named tasks (set up a profile, draft a lead magnet, build a landing page, prepare a webinar) with an hour estimate and a week number. A Gantt-style horizontal timeline is the simplest way to see whether the plan is realistic before anyone commits to it.

#### A budget by tactic

Most of a lean program's cost is team hours, not media spend — direct mail and paid retargeting are usually the only hard line items; everything else (content, landing pages, webinar production) is labor. Total the hours and the dollars separately, and check both against what the team can actually absorb in the window.

## Worked example: a Fortune 500 industrial client

*Anonymized — the in-class capstone example used a real training-provider client's actual campaign name, target-account list, task-level hours, and budget figures in the client's own currency. None of that is reproduced here; the numbers below are illustrative and round, and the client is described only as a generic industrial equipment manufacturer, exactly like Module 1 anonymized its own real-client workshop example into a generic "B2B shared-services provider."*

The scenario: a "Plant Efficiency Audit" campaign aimed at 20 target accounts, with the goal of generating at least 8 opportunities worth $400,000 in pipeline. Content plan: 3 lead magnets, 10 draft social posts, two 10-email nurture sequences, 20 personalized content hubs, and one webinar with its own landing page.

| Metric | Now | +1 month | +3 months | +6 months |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Engaged chief engineers / plant managers | 0 | 8 | 12 | 15 |
| Completed plant-efficiency audits | 0 | 1 | 4 | 7 |
| Opportunities in pipeline | 0 | 0 | 3 | 8 |
| Pipeline value | $0 | $0 | $150,000 | $400,000 |

The execution plan behind those numbers: 16 tasks and 182 hours laid out across a 6-week calendar, with an illustrative $9,500 budget — direct mail production (~$4,000) and retargeting media (~$5,500) are the only hard media costs; the rest is team hours across marketing, an account manager, an SDR, and a sales lead. The full, fill-in version of this plan — with the RACI columns, the weekly calendar, and an open action-items log — is the downloadable budget & RACI template above.

## Running the program: team roles & meeting cadence

The minimal ABM team is three roles, not more: **Marketing** (content, events, advertising), an **SDR** (research, warm-up, nurture), and **Sales** (negotiation, relationship, customer success). A meeting cadence is what keeps those three roles from drifting apart: a **weekly** 5-10 minute 1:1 between the marketer and each SDR/rep to sync on named accounts, a **monthly** metrics-and-plan review across the whole team, and a **quarterly business review** with leadership invited, covering results and any changes to the target-account list.

Keys to keeping sales and marketing aligned once the cadence is running: start with one joint kickoff meeting explaining why anything needs to change; agree shared definitions in writing for what counts as a qualified account or a sales-ready opportunity (research cited in class found only 44% of companies had formally agreed this); tie part of marketing's bonus to pipeline created and won, not just leads delivered; include marketing in forecasting calls; run a regular joint account-review meeting with clear ground rules (some teams call these "quarterback meetings"); agree an SLA for research quality and response time; and get visible executive sponsorship before rollout — ABM is an organizational change, and it meets resistance without it.

## Exercises

#### Homework 1 — draft your own ABM program

Pick one account tier from your Module 2 shortlist (or a single 1:1 account) and work through all four phases — warm-up, engagement, nurture, acceleration. For each phase, select 2-3 tactics from this module's tactics lists and write down why they fit this account tier specifically. List every touchpoint in sequence with a rough week number, using the 6-week timeline above as a template. Estimate hours per task and assign exactly one Accountable owner to each, using RACI rather than a plain to-do list. Sanity-check the total hours against who you actually have available before committing to the calendar.

#### Homework 2 — fill in the budget & RACI template

Open `program-budget-raci-template.xlsx` (linked above) and clear every example value on the Budget & RACI sheet. Enter your own program's tasks, hours, and illustrative or real budget figures, one row per task. Assign R/A/C/I using your own team's role labels — don't leave any task without exactly one Accountable owner. Fill in the Program Setup sheet with your target-account count, campaign name, goal, and KPIs. Log at least 3 real open questions or blockers on the Action Items sheet before you call the plan final.

#### Homework 3 — design your internal rollout plan

Decide which rollout pattern fits your org: a full workshop-and-pilot-team launch, or a lightweight 3-account pilot with one enthusiastic colleague. If piloting: pick exactly 3 target accounts, personalize the touchpoints for their real stakeholders, and set a 90-day review date. Book the recurring meetings now, not later — a weekly stand-up, a monthly metrics review, and a quarterly business review with leadership invited. Write one paragraph on what "good" looks like after 90 days; that paragraph becomes the yardstick for Homework 4.

#### Homework 4 — run one week of the program through MegaBrain

Using the MegaBrain account you set up in Module 1, feed your Homework 1 touchpoint list into an agent prompt asking for a scheduled program with owners. Compare the agent's draft plan against your own manual draft and note every place they disagree. Pick the single biggest disagreement and resolve it by hand — that's the judgment call an agent can't make for you. Keep both drafts; you'll want the comparison the first time a stakeholder asks why the plan looks the way it does.
