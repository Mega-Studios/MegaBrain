# Selecting Target Accounts

*Module 02 · Selecting Target Accounts*

Build an Ideal Customer Profile, score and rank accounts, and turn a long list of possible customers into a short list worth a dedicated program.

Two pieces of the original material aren't reproduced here: a real client's internal target-account spreadsheet (real company names, real employee contact details) has been replaced throughout with a fictional illustrative example, and a real vendor's contact-enrichment API contact card has been dropped entirely. Examples have also been adapted from the original Russian market to Western companies and tools.

## Set the program goal before you touch a target list

The module opens with a warning, not a framework: don't build a target-account list before you've picked a goal, because the list looks different depending on the answer. "Win new mid-market logos" and "grow existing accounts through cross-sell and upsell" are different programs, built from different starting lists, scored on different criteria. Other goal shapes that show up in real programs: shortening the sales cycle by engaging every buying-committee member at once instead of one contact at a time; expanding into a new department inside an account you already have a foothold in; improving win rate by getting executive sponsors involved earlier; reducing churn with proactive, personalized account plans; or breaking into a new geography. Whatever you pick, write it as a number — "open a conversation with 40 target accounts, close 2 pilot deals" — not as an aspiration like "raise awareness."

There's a concrete reason to pick the goal first: winning a new logo through direct sales takes roughly **14 times** the effort of expanding an existing account through a direct upsell, according to the sales-motion comparison the class walks through (new-logo-via-partner, renewal-via-partner, your own e-commerce platform, and third-party marketplaces all fall somewhere in between). If your program goal is new-logo acquisition, budget for it to take longer and cost more per account than an expansion motion would — that's the expected shape of the problem, not evidence that something in your program is broken.

### Pilot programs: the rule of one

The single most common first-time-ABM mistake the instructors flag is running a pilot across too many industries, tiers, and reps simultaneously. The fix is a deliberately narrow pilot:

- **One industry.** Not a portfolio of verticals.
- **One account tier** — cluster/"ABM Lite," for example — not a mix of strategic and programmatic accounts.
- **One relationship stage** — all cold, or all post-first-meeting, or all upsell — not a blend.
- **One marketer, one sales rep, one goal, one primary tactic.**
- **Cap the pilot at 20 target accounts** maximum. Run a second pilot once the first one proves the model, rather than scaling scope before you've proven the motion works at all.

## The Ideal Customer Profile (ICP), and why you need one

An Ideal Customer Profile answers a narrower question than most teams think it does. The class is explicit about the distinction: *we don't need an answer to who COULD buy from us — we need an answer to who WILL WANT to buy from us.* Five concrete reasons an ICP earns its place before you build a list:

- **Focus.** If the entire addressable market fits your profile, the exercise changed nothing — a working ICP should visibly narrow the field.
- **Personalization.** Industry-, company-, and persona-level personalization (the subject of Module 5) all start from the ICP; without one, "personalization" collapses to a first-name merge tag.
- **Channel selection.** Different ICPs often live on different channels — an ICP tells you where to actually spend effort.
- **Qualification.** A defined ICP turns "does this feel like a good fit?" into a checklist a discovery call can actually run through.
- **It's the direct input to the scoring model** you'll build later in this module — you can't weight what you haven't first defined.

You're allowed to run more than one ICP at a time — just make sure each one has its own program behind it, rather than one list trying to serve two different goals at once. And build the ICP as a team: if sales and marketing don't both sign off on it, that's a sign the exercise wasn't actually collaborative — and the most common failure mode in this whole module is marketing handing sales a target-account list nobody in sales actually believes in.

## Six factor groups behind every ICP

The class breaks ICP criteria into six factor groups, plus a seventh check that isn't scored at all — disqualifying factors, which remove a candidate regardless of how well it scores everywhere else.

### Demographic factors

The most familiar layer: employee count and revenue band; a ceiling above which an account becomes too large for you to serve well (yes, a maximum, not just a minimum); growth-rate segment (hyper-growth vs. stable); industry vertical and sub-vertical; and HQ plus branch locations.

### Structural factors

How the company is organized and equipped, which the class treats as binary rather than scored on a scale — a company either has the attribute or it doesn't, and that clean split is what makes structural factors useful for separating a "dream client" from one not worth chasing: process maturity relevant to your product; independent company vs. regional subsidiary; market leader vs. fast-follower; centralized vs. decentralized decision-making; already using a competitor's product (or, conversely, a visible gap your product could fill); and growth by acquisition vs. organic growth.

### Behavioral and situational factors

Behavioral factors need actual research, not a database export: which department really holds budget power — operations, finance, or IT; what that department is measured and rewarded on; and how open the company is to working with unproven vendors versus only established brands. Situational factors — often called "trigger events" or "compelling events" — are changes in the account's circumstances: new leadership, a merger or acquisition, a funding round, a product launch or territorial expansion, a hiring wave, or a regulatory change. The class cites a widely-used stat here: roughly **75% of deals go to whichever vendor helps the buyer name the right solution to their problem first** — situational signals are what tell you when to show up.

### Social proximity

Warm paths into an account shorten the distance to a first real conversation: former colleagues of yours who now work at the target account; mutual connections (on LinkedIn or elsewhere) who could make an introduction; shared alumni networks or professional communities; and an internal champion who's already quietly advocating for you. An advisory board built around senior, well-connected industry figures is one structured way teams manufacture this proximity deliberately rather than waiting for it to occur naturally.

### Intent — three tiers of "demonstrated interest"

Intent data comes in three tiers of decreasing trust and increasing reach:

| Tier | Source | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| 1st-party | Your own site and CRM — known or anonymous visitors | Free, highest-trust, smallest reach |
| 2nd-party | Data purchased directly from a partner (e.g. G2's buyer-intent feed) | Higher cost, still fairly trustworthy |
| 3rd-party | Cooperative/exchange data from providers such as 6sense or Bombora | Broadest reach, noisiest signal — good for widening the net, not for a final call |

Other intent proxies worth tracking directly: total minutes of engagement (call length, time on-site, email read time), number of existing contacts for the account already in your CRM, breadth of products or content consumed, and the number of channels an account already uses to interact with you.

### Disqualifying factors

Every business needs its own list, but common patterns from the class: no named budget owner and no near-term plan to create one; a strong build-vs-buy culture with an in-house team already covering the same ground; an account so large a single logo would consume more delivery capacity than you can safely commit; a product surface that's too narrow (or too broad and undifferentiated — "we do everything") to map to a real workflow; no CTO or technical decision-maker role at all, or one who's very new and unproven internally; and a multi-year contract already locked in with a direct competitor.

## Formalizing the ICP

Once the six factor groups are populated, the class pushes teams to write the ICP down in two forms: a structured table (factor → ideal / acceptable / exclude, exactly the shape used in the downloadable template above) and a plain-language paragraph a salesperson could read cold and immediately recognize a good-fit account from a bad one. A useful discipline check before you ship an ICP: does everyone on the team actually agree with it (if not, the exercise wasn't really collaborative); is it simple enough to work from — the class suggests roughly 12 attributes is plenty; does it actually narrow the market, rather than describing "anyone who could plausibly buy"; is every factor something you can realistically find data for (factors that require a live conversation belong in your discovery-call script, not the ICP); and do you need more than one ICP, each with its own program, rather than forcing one list to do two jobs.

## Sourcing the raw account list

With the ICP defined, the next step is deliberately loose: build a raw candidate list of roughly **20–50 accounts** that broadly match the demographic and structural factors, without over-filtering yet — precision scoring happens in the next step. The class surveys where that raw list comes from, organized here by category and updated to current (July 2026) tool names:

| Category | Example tools |
|---|---|
| Contact & company databases | LinkedIn Sales Navigator, ZoomInfo, Apollo.io |
| Firmographic / look-alike modeling | Keyplay, Ocean.io, Clay |
| Market & funding intelligence | Crunchbase, Dealroom.co, PitchBook |
| Technographic lookup (what software a site runs) | BuiltWith, Wappalyzer |
| Intent data | 6sense, Bombora, G2 Buyer Intent |
| Contact enrichment & verification | RocketReach, Breeze Intelligence (HubSpot's post-acquisition rebrand of Clearbit), Enrow |

Look-alike modeling tools are worth calling out specifically: give them a handful of your best existing customers and they return a ranked list of companies that resemble them on firmographic and technographic signals — a faster starting point than building filters from scratch. The class also covers lighter-weight sourcing channels worth checking off a list once the primary databases are exhausted: industry rankings and registries, trade shows and conference exhibitor lists, marketplaces and product aggregators, industry associations, your own partners' and distributors' client rosters, and (carefully, and within each site's terms of service) web scraping for structured public data.

## Scoring and prioritizing the list

With a raw list in hand, the class scores every candidate on two axes — **account attractiveness** and **your competitive/business position** — and plots the result on a GE/McKinsey-style 3×3 grid, the same tool used for portfolio decisions in general strategy work:

- Top-right (high attractiveness, strong position): invest for growth — this is Strategic ABM territory.
- The diagonal middle band: grow selectively, usually with Programmatic ABM rather than a bespoke motion.
- Bottom-left (low attractiveness, weak position): don't spend real budget here — hold, or divest the relationship to plain segmented mass marketing.

Getting there requires an actual scoring model — a short list of weighted factors for each axis, each with defined 1/3/5-point bands so different people score the same account the same way. A starting version, adapted from the class (adjust the factors and weights to your own business):

| Factor | Weight | 1 point | 3 points | 5 points |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Our business position** | | | | |
| Industry fit | 0.5 | Commodity / raw materials | Capital equipment | Software / high-growth tech |
| Geography | 0.2 | Secondary market | Regional hub | Major metro / core market |
| Relationship warmth | 0.3 | No connection | 1st/2nd-degree LinkedIn | Personally acquainted |
| **Account attractiveness** | | | | |
| YoY revenue growth | 0.1 | Declining | Up to 10% | Over 10% |
| Digital maturity (site + email) | 0.3 | Neither in place | One of the two | Both, actively used |
| Agency / consultant experience | 0.3 | Never worked with one | Worked with one once | Ongoing relationship |
| Staff with relevant skills on payroll | 0.3 | None | One person | More than one |

If you genuinely don't know a factor's value for a given account, give every account on the list the same default score for that factor rather than guessing account-by-account — an inconsistent guess is worse than a consistent placeholder. The downloadable ICP & scoring template above builds this model out with formulas that total each axis and plot the result automatically.

Three things the class flags about running the final cut, all more about team process than spreadsheet mechanics: run it as a joint sales-and-marketing session, not two separate exercises reconciled after the fact; watch specifically for accounts with a reputation as difficult or even abusive to work with, however attractive they score on paper; and expect the whole exercise to take noticeably longer than you budgeted — that's usually a sign you're doing it properly, not that something has gone wrong.

## Two published account-selection frameworks worth studying

The class references two frameworks from outside the training itself — both summarized here rather than reproduced in full, since they're each other companies' original published material:

- **OneLogin's public ICP framework** scores an account across five columns: Technical Attributes (the SaaS and identity/access-management stack already in place), Business Attributes (digital-transformation initiatives, workforce distribution), Trigger / Compelling Event (a large-scale app deployment, a security incident), Product/Capability Interest (single sign-on at scale, multi-factor authentication), and Pain Points (fragmented identity management, shadow IT). It's a clean example of mapping ICP factors directly to what the sales conversation will actually be about — worth finding and reading in full if the shape maps to your own product.
- **Fabiana Carpio Brunetti**, then Director of Global ABM at Pure Storage, has published an account-selection model that goes past ICP fit alone, scoring five factors: strategic alignment, revenue potential, engagement opportunity (essentially social proximity), operational readiness, and the influence of an internal champion. Her core argument, worth carrying into your own scoring model: anchor the list in data, not just in which accounts the sales team would personally enjoy selling into.

## Agentic ABM in practice

Two agent-shaped ways to speed up this module's work, mirrored in the deck:

- **MegaBrain gateway workflow.** Feed an agent your raw account list and your weighted scoring model in a single prompt — "score these 200 accounts against our ICP and rank the top 30" — routed through MegaBrain's model-agnostic gateway, and get back a ranked shortlist with a written scoring rationale per account. The workshop described above is still worth running to sanity-check the output, but the mechanical scoring pass that used to eat an afternoon now takes one prompt.
- **Other tools mapped to this module's tasks:** **Clay** — pull firmographic, technographic and funding data per account and run your weighted formula across the whole raw list automatically. **ZoomInfo Copilot** — AI-assisted account and buying-group identification layered on top of ZoomInfo's own database. A **Perplexity or other deep-research agent** — given a seed list of your best customers, research and draft a first-pass ICP narrative. **n8n or Make** — a scheduled workflow that automatically re-scores your account list every month as firmographic and intent data refreshes.

## Exercises

#### Homework 1 — score the attributes of your ideal client

Using the best-10-15 and worst-10-15 existing-customer lists from Module 1, run a manual scoring pass across all six factor groups above for every account on both lists. For your best customers, answer: why did they choose you over competitors, what was happening inside their company around the time they bought, and who was actually involved in the buying process? Do the same for your worst customers, then write down every factor where the best and worst groups clearly diverge — that divergence is the raw material your ICP gets built from. The downloadable worksheet above gives you the exact columns to fill in.

#### Homework 2 — build your raw account list

Using the demographic and structural factors that came out of Homework 1, build a raw candidate list of 20–50 target accounts. Pull from at least two different source categories in the sourcing table above — e.g. LinkedIn Sales Navigator plus one look-alike modeling tool — rather than relying on a single database. For every account, capture at minimum: company name, domain, industry, employee-count band, and HQ location. Keep the filter loose here on purpose; tighter filtering happens in Homeworks 3 and 4.

#### Homework 3 — build your own scoring model

Pick 3–5 factors for account attractiveness and 3–5 factors for your own business position, starting from the example table above and adjusting factors and weights to fit your business. Assign weights so attractiveness factors sum to 1.0, and business-position factors separately sum to 1.0. For every factor, write down what a 1-point, 3-point, and 5-point answer actually looks like — vague bands produce inconsistent scores between people. If you don't yet know a factor's value for a given account, give every account the same default score for it rather than guessing.

#### Homework 4 — plot the matrix and cut the pilot shortlist

Score every account on your raw list against the model from Homework 3, and plot each one on the GE/McKinsey grid (the downloadable template does this automatically once your scores are in). As a team — not solo — identify your 3–10 highest-priority accounts for the ABM pilot. Every account on the pilot shortlist needs to share the same relationship stage (all cold, or all post-meeting, or all upsell) and the same segment or industry; a mixed shortlist is exactly what the "rule of one" earlier in this module is warning against. Bring the finished shortlist and scoring model into Module 3, where you'll start building account intelligence on every name on the list.
